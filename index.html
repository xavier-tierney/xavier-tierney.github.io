Job Market Paper

City Shape: Costs and Responses with Evidence from Sydney

Abstract

Many cities are settled near geographic boundaries such as harbors or mountains. As they grow, they are forced to spread into distorted, non-compact urban footprints, with longer travel times than circular cities. Moreover, if the city sprawls in one direction, building investments and agglomeration forces can leave the center of business stranded at the far end of the city. As low-income residents are most likely to be pushed to the periphery, the resulting costs of reduced access to the center may be most directly borne by the worst off. In this paper, I bring evidence on the costs and possible responses to these effects of distorted city shape using a structural model calibrated to Sydney. First, I perform stylised counterfactuals to estimate the costs and their incidence, finding that bad shape can substantially worsen commute times, wages and output, and can contribute to inequality. I then estimate the effects of a government policy to shift public service jobs to areas with lower employment access.
