I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the London School of Economics. My research is in urban and spatial economics. I study how the spatial structure of the city impacts workers, and whether place-based policies can shape it. My work combines quantitative spatial equilibrium models with quasi-experimental evidence.

Job Market Paper

Lopsided Cities: Employment-Residence Mismatches and Place-Based Responses

Abstract

Many cities have lopsided employment distributions: the center of business is tied to one side of the city, while residences sprawl out in the other direction. This means average distances to employment are further than they would be in a symmetric city – increasing commute times. In this paper, I investigate the costs of such lopsidedness, its impact on inequality and possible policy responses. Calibrating a quantitative urban model to Sydney, I show that a counterfactual city with a more centralised employment hub could have several percentage points higher output and worker welfare, particularly for the low-skill workers currently far from the existing center. I then investigate the effects of a government policy to try to directly alter the spatial distribution of employment: a 2018 policy to shift over 10,000 government jobs to a more central location in Sydney. Using a synthetic control method, I find that land values surrounding the new offices increased substantially compared to comparable locations, while private employment responses were initially muted, except for some high-skill sectors. I then estimate a dynamic quantitative urban model, showing that the initial land value impacts are consistent with significant spillovers to the private sector, but that employment responses may be delayed by construction lags and sticky labour markets.
